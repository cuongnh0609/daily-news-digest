C1Nadella: Treat AI Models as Insider Risks — 7 Design Principles for Enterprise AI Governance

📰 Article (English)

Microsoft CEO Satya Nadella published a detailed essay on X titled "Models as Insider Risks in the Super Intelligence Era," arguing that frontier AI models should be governed using the same frameworks enterprises apply to privileged human insiders. His core argument is not that models are malicious, but that any sufficiently capable actor with access to sensitive systems — whether human or AI — can make mistakes or be compromised. He points out that companies are granting models access to critical data and mission-critical operations even though model behavior cannot be traced to specific training data or weights, and that the AI provider cannot be held responsible for enterprise-level decisions. Nadella's seven design principles for enterprise AI governance are: (1) model diversity to avoid single-point-of-failure, (2) full observability with tamper-evident logs, (3) continuous end-to-end testing including adversarial scenarios, (4) independent human-controlled permission management outside the model itself, (5) audits separated from the model being audited, (6) containment mechanisms allowing humans to halt a model mid-task, and (7) timely incident disclosure. The essay frames AI safety as an organizational and architectural challenge rather than a purely technical one.

C2MASS: Recursive Self-Improvement via Collective Intelligence — Sakana AI's Multi-Agent Training Loop

📰 Article (English)

Sakana AI has published MASS (Multi-Agent Self-improvement System), a research project exploring homogeneous recursive self-improvement via collective intelligence. In the MASS loop, a single model simultaneously plays three roles: task solver, workflow optimizer, and pairwise evaluator. The system searches for better multi-agent team workflows using the model's own comparative judgments, then fine-tunes on the highest-quality team execution trajectories — with the workflow itself removed from the training prompt to prevent memorization. After two optimization rounds on a Qwen3.6-27B base model, MASS showed higher win rates on held-out research tasks and 1.2–1.6× improvement in score per output token on several research benchmarks. However, gains did not carry over to two software-engineering benchmarks, suggesting domain-specific limits. The research team identified a notable risk: shared self-judgment blind spots. Because all agents share the same base model and thus the same systematic biases, errors in the evaluator role could reinforce themselves across the entire multi-agent system — a concern with direct implications for production agentic workflows. The team suggests multi-agent training trajectories offer denser training signal than single-agent ones, and that the optimizer role may matter more than the evaluator role in driving improvement.

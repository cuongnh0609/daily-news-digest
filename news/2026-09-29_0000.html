📰 Article (English)

Alex Ewerlöf's widely-shared post challenges the narrative that AI has "solved" coding, arguing this reflects a fundamental misunderstanding of professional software engineering. While LLMs excel at code generation, they consistently fail at the aspects that dominate production environments: maintaining reliability, security, and long-term scalability. The author identifies five core problems. First, code creation is cheap; what costs money is maintenance and non-functional requirements that dominate production systems. Ewerlöf concludes that LLMs are powerful accelerators for skilled engineers but cannot replace the judgment, accountability, and systemic thinking that professional coding requires.

C3Malleable Software: Restoring User Agency in a World of Locked-Down Apps — Ink & Switch Essay

📰 Article (English)

Ink & Switch's widely-discussed essay argues for "malleable software" — computing systems where users can adapt tools to their specific needs without friction, rather than passively accepting locked-down applications. The core thesis is that current mass-produced software treats users as passive recipients, forcing them to adapt workflows to tools rather than the other way around. The essay proposes three principles: a "gentle slope" from user to creator (like spreadsheets, which let users progress from passive consumption to deep customization without cliff-like skill jumps); "tools over apps" (composable data-sharing tools replacing monolithic applications that cannot interoperate); and "communal creation" (local communities collaboratively building solutions tailored to their specific contexts). The authors demonstrate several prototypes: PushPin and Patchwork for real-time collaborative document editing, Cambria for live data schema migration between tools, and Potluck/Embark for dynamic document creation. The essay's relevance for engineers is its challenge to prioritize user agency and extensibility over surface polish — specifically by supporting permissionless modification, shared open data formats, and lightweight composition rather than building "food courts" where users must eat what's served.

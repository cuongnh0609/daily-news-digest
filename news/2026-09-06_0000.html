📰 Article (English)

Engineer and writer Sylvain Kalache examines a growing paradox in modern incident response: as AI tools become increasingly capable at resolving routine operational incidents automatically, the human engineers responsible for those systems are progressively losing hands-on familiarity with how those systems actually work. Kalache draws on Lisanne Bainbridge's seminal 1983 research on "automation's ironies," which showed that automation reduces operators' practice of routine work while leaving them responsible for the most complex, unfamiliar failures — exactly when human expertise matters most. His prediction: while average incident resolution time will improve, response time for genuinely complex incidents will worsen because engineers won't have accumulated the intuitive experience necessary to investigate novel failure modes. He draws a parallel with aviation's approach to this exact problem: pilots rarely encounter catastrophic failures in real life but train extensively in simulators to maintain readiness. His prescription is incident simulation as standard engineering practice — regular tabletop exercises, chaos engineering drills, and deliberate hands-on system interaction. The underlying concept is "comprehension debt": the growing gap between how a system actually functions and what engineers understand about it.

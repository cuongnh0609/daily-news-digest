📰 Article (English)

Zed editor's co-founder Nathan Sobo published a provocative essay arguing that Ted Nelson's 1965 "Project Xanadu" — a vision for a hypertext system that never copies, never overwrites, and maintains provenance links forever — was impossible until now not because the idea was wrong, but because the right kind of user never existed. Nelson's principles were radical: transclusion instead of copying, permanent versioning, and every link tracking its original source. The web abandoned these ideals for simplicity, leaving us with brittle links that break when targets move. The missing piece, Sobo argues, was an actor who could "follow more layers of subtext than people can hold in their heads at once, including quotation sources, discussions, and code traces." That actor is the AI agent. Agents can patiently traverse every link, every time, without fatigue — making them the "perfect user" for xanalogical computing. Zed's Delta platform now provides the infrastructure Nelson lacked: Lamport timestamps for operation naming, Merkle trees for immutable content, CRDTs for distributed editing, and cheap storage enabling version permanence. Delta threads capture conversations and code together as permanent structured history. Sobo frames this as not just a technical milestone but a philosophical one: after sixty years, Nelson's dream finally finds its ideal audience in machines that can traverse complete provenance and reasoning chains.

C2Cloudflare Discloses Cross-Request KV Cache Exposure in GLM-5.3 on Workers AI — 155 Responses Leaked Other Users' Data

📰 Article (English)

Cloudflare has disclosed a cross-request KV cache exposure affecting the GLM-5.3 model (@cf/zai-org/glm-5.3) on its Workers AI platform. Security researcher Gadi Evron reported the issue on September 8, 2026 after discovering that long prompts were occasionally returning data from other customers' recent requests. The exposure window lasted from GLM-5.3's August 27 launch until September 9 when the model was taken offline. Cloudflare confirmed 155 affected responses, all involving prompts exceeding 681,280 tokens, and found no evidence of deliberate exploitation. The root cause was a combination of two issues. First, GLM-5.3's 1,048,576-token context window exceeded the KV pool capacity of 681,280 tokens. Second, a bug in SGLang's disaggregated prefill/decode mode mishandled over-length requests — rejected requests received a one-token placeholder but still passed admission checks, causing the decode node to attend over stale KV pages from earlier requests while returning HTTP 200. Fixes include expanding the KV pool to ~2.04M tokens, patching SGLang (upstream PRs 36988 and 38935) so over-length requests fail at admission, and deployment-time checks comparing pool size to model context length. Cloudflare plans to add page-level integrity tags and formal verification of its RDMA-based KV transfer protocol. The takeaway for teams running inference infrastructure: KV pages are not zeroed between requests — page ownership tracking across nodes is essential.

C3The Mythical Man-Month in the Age of AI — Brooks's Law Hasn't Disappeared, It Has Just Changed Form

📰 Article (English)

Jan van den Berg revisits Frederick Brooks's 1975 observation that "adding manpower to a late software project makes it later" — and asks whether AI agents have finally broken this law. At first glance, the answer seems yes: agents onboard instantly, never need a coffee break, and bypass the overhead of stand-ups, onboarding, and office politics. But van den Berg argues the law has not vanished. It has merely changed form. His three key arguments are sharp. First, sequential work stays sequential — the "Baby Problem" shows nine agents still cannot produce a baby in one month. Some tasks fundamentally resist parallelization regardless of how many workers (human or AI) are available. Second, overhead has shifted rather than disappeared. The coordinator now manages agents instead of people, but coordination cost remains. Third, unstructured parallelism creates churn: multiple autonomous agents working on the same codebase without clear orchestration can endlessly rewrite each other's work. Van den Berg's updated version of Brooks's Law: throwing agents at an ill-defined project only produces wrong answers faster. The implication for engineering teams is clear — before scaling with AI agents, invest in clear requirements, task decomposition, and human oversight architecture. Spec-driven development and well-defined agent orchestration patterns are not optional extras; they are prerequisites for productive multi-agent systems.
